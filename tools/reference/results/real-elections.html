How a batch defeat lines up

Many official counts defeat several hopeless candidates at once, or stop as soon as someone has a majority. Neither changes the rounds passed through, so each official round is required to be one of this count's rounds, in order, with the same candidates still in it. Known differences

Every figure that doesn't match, by cause. In each the same candidates are eliminated, in the same order, and the same candidates win, unless it says otherwise.

The ballot record isn't quite the ballots counted

A few votes a candidate apart from the first round on, some over and some under, under every reading of the ballots tried.

  • Alameda County: 4 Oakland contests of 2024–2025, 1–7 votes a candidate.
  • Alaska: 9 contests of 2022–2024, first rounds exact, later rounds 1–9 votes a candidate. The Division of Elections' own 2022 export, read anew, gives the same figures in the four of 2022, so FairVote's copies of them are faithful; no reading of the ballots tried closes the gaps, and the cause isn't shown.
  • New York City: 4 primaries of 2021, 2–19 votes a candidate. The Board's own published ballots give these figures, so they aren't quite the ballots it counted: their first choices are 2, 2 and 14 short of the Board's first round in three, and in District 9 one short with votes moved both ways (up to 6 a candidate).
  • Maine: the 2018 Democratic primary for governor, Janet Mills 3 votes higher in the last round.
  • Maine: the 2026 Democratic primaries for Congress in the 2nd District and for governor, and the Republican primary for governor, 3–17 votes a candidate. Their published exports aren't quite the ballots the State counted: their first rankings differ from the State's own tally of first choices in 9, 13 and 17 places (14, 38 and 27 ballots), where the two primaries that match every round agree with it in every place.
  • Minneapolis: 2009 Board of Estimate, Becker 16,727 here against 16,728; every later round matches.

The official results checked aren't the final ones

Only an earlier version survives in the Internet Archive.

  • San Francisco: 2024 city attorney, district attorney and sheriff, captured 22–27 November, before certification on 3 December; 76–80 votes short.
  • Alaska: the August 2022 special for US House, the unofficial report of 31 August; 54–71 votes.

Ties drawn by lot

A tie for exclusion the rules leave to lot, where this calculator's declared order chose the other candidate. The same winners either way.

  • District 1: David Linn and Peggy Sue Owens, tied on 1,441.
  • District 3: Jaclyn Smith-Moore and Patrick Hilton, tied on 200.
  • NSW: 15 councillor counts, from Armidale to Wingecarribee; the Commission's draw and the declared order part at the tie, and the same councillors are elected.

Slips and gaps in the published sources

Where the source contradicts itself or lacks what the count needs.

  • Eastpointe 2021: round 4 gives Rob Baker 1,058.8175, 2 more than its own round 3 plus transfers; its round 5 builds on 1,056.8175, as here.
  • Cambridge 2019–2023: the records don't mark overvotes, so first counts are a few votes off and only winners are compared. The 2023 School Committee's last exclusion was King, 8 votes behind Harding; here it's Harding, 12 behind.
  • Scotland: of Wikipedia's 1,043 result boxes, 169 differ somewhere. Where an official source exists, the box is the one in error.
  • Scotland, Midlothian 2012 ward 1: the ballot file truncates surnames, so its winners were matched to the declared ones by hand.
  • Scotland, East Lothian 2017 ward 3: Wikipedia's result box gives 3 seats for 4; Denis Mollison's recount of the ballots confirms this count's winners.
